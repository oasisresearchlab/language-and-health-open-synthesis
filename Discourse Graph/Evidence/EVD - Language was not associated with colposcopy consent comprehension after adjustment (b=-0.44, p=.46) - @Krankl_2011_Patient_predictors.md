## Description

![[Krankl_2011_Patient_predictors-table3.png]]

After adjusting for confounding variables (notably education and other socioeconomic factors) in a robust multivariate linear regression, language was NOT significantly associated with colposcopy comprehension (β = −0.44; 95% CI −1.62 to 0.73; p = .46) (Table 3). This is a null result: the large unadjusted English–Spanish gap disappeared once confounders were controlled, and the authors caution that the sign and magnitude of β cannot be interpreted because the change is not significant.

> "Despite lower scores on the colposcopy knowledge survey among Spanish-speaking subjects, after controlling for confounding factors in multivariate analysis, language was not significantly associated with diminished comprehension (β = −0.44; p = .46). The value and direction of β cannot be interpreted because the change in summary colposcopy knowledge score is not significant." (Krankl, 2011, p. 83)

> "Using linear regression to adjust for confounding variables, we found that language was not significantly associated with greater understanding (p = .46)." (Krankl, 2011, p. 80)

## Methods Context

### What?

The observable: the adjusted (independent) effect of language group on the summary colposcopy knowledge score, expressed as a regression coefficient with 95% CI.

> "Multivariate model: adjusted effect of language and education … Language −0.44 −1.62 to 0.73 .46" (Krankl, 2011, p. 84)

### How?

Robust multivariate linear regression starting from language as the covariate of interest, adding each a priori confounder (retained if p < .05) to estimate language's absolute effect on the outcome.

> "Multivariate analysis was used to examine the relationship between subject understanding of colposcopy and multiple potential predictive variables using robust linear regression." (Krankl, 2011, p. 82)

### Who?

The 149 complete-data colposcopy patients (111 English, 38 Spanish speakers) at two Boston hospitals; reference category for language was speaking Spanish.

> "This study actually accrued 111 English speakers and 38 Spanish speakers." (Krankl, 2011, p. 82)
