In  this  single-institution  study at HSS, we found no disparity in RA utilization based on English proficiency. Our study did demon-strate  increased  odds  of  RA  utilization  with  female  sex,  Asian  race,  public  insurance,  and  recent  year  of  surgery  and  decreased  odds  with  higher  ASA  status  and inpatient surgery status.Raymond  et  al  published  a  study  examining  the  effect of primary spoken language on the use of RA in patients undergoing primary joint arthroplasty. They demonstrated  that  non-English,  non-Spanish  speak-ers were less likely to receive RA than English speak-ers.2  The  difference  in  results  from  their  study  when  compared to ours is complex, likely contingent on dif-ferences  in  patient  population,  preoperative  encoun-ters,  and  an  interplay  with  surgical  colleagues.  The  patient  population  at  HSS  compared  to  other  New  York  area  hospitals  greatly  influences  these  results.  When comparing our study population to New York hospitals who partake in the comprehensive care for joint  replacement  (CJR)  bundle  payment  program  for  total  joint  replacements,  we  see  a  higher  num-ber  with  commercial  insurance  (52%  vs  40%),  fewer  patients   with   Medicaid/Medicare   (47%   vs   48%),   and fewer patients who are self-payers or uninsured (1.5%  vs  11%).5  Another  key  factor  that  likely  influ-ences the preoperative experience for LEP patients at our institution is the Language Service program. This program provides translation services during preop-erative  clinic  appointments  with  language-specific  after-visit summaries, as well as ensuring translation in the perioperative period on the day of surgery. This service was available to patients throughout the study period. Though we do not have access to the conver-sations our surgical colleagues or preoperative clinic physicians had with patients, these frequently involve discussion  of  anesthetic  options,  which  would  have  included   mention   of   RA,   and   are   conducted   in   patients’  preferred  language.  It  is  possible  that  these  services  play  a  role  in  mitigating  differences  seen  in  RA utilization for LEP patients.Limitations  of  this  study  include  its  retrospec-tive  nature,  single-institution  data,  and  variability  in  provider  bias.  Additionally,  we  were  unable  to  exclude  patients  with  cognitive  impairment  from  our  dataset  as  we  did  not  have  access  to  this  vari-able  in  our  review.  Finally,  despite  our  large  sam-ple size, post hoc analysis reveals that the study is underpowered  to  detect  the  observed,  nonsignifi-cant effect size, which is clinically irrelevant. While post hoc power calculations are not recommended for  definitive  conclusions,  they  highlight  poten-tial  power  issues.  Although  the  study  is  designed  to  detect  larger,  clinically  meaningful  differences  of 2% or more, the current results should be inter-preted  cautiously,  as  the  study  might  overlook  a  significant difference that exists.Further  research  and  collaboration  are  needed  to  determine why these differences between institutions exist, and how to best close the gap in RA utilization for LEP patients nationally."
