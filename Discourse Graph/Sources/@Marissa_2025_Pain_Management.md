title: Pain Management Among People with Limited English Proficiency Treated by Emergency Medical Services
journal: Journal of general internal medicine
pubmed_id: 40425934
abstract: |
  Among the estimated 16 million emergency medical services (EMS) encounters in the USA annually, 20–30% of patients report moderate-to-severe pain and 10% report severe pain, yet only 40% of encounters result in clinically significant pain reduction.1 Gaps in identifying and treating pain may be greater for people with limited English proficiency (LEP) and resulting communication barriers. In 2019, 67.8 million people in the USA reported speaking a language other than English at home2 and 46.2 million (13.9%) Americans are foreign-born.3 It is therefore imperative to examine EMS care among people with LEP and, as a key indicator of care quality, assess for gaps in pain management. Neither has been examined previously in the general population; thus, we report differences in pain assessment and management by EMS among LEP and non-LEP patients treated by a large, advanced life support ambulance service in Minnesota and Wisconsin.

outcomes: |
  We identified 89,832 runs for non-LEP and 1122 runs for LEP patients (Table 1). There were fewer pain assessments in LEP (median 1, IQR [0, 2]) compared to non-LEP (median 2, IQR [1,2]) patients (p < 0.001). Use of language line interpreter did not improve the number of pain assessments (p = 0.71). Administration rates of aspirin (0.80% vs. 2.19%, p = 0.002), nitroglycerin (1.60% vs. 2.58%, p = 0.05), ketorolac (0.27% vs. 0.94%, p = 0.03), and fentanyl/hydromorphone (4.37% vs. 7.95%, p < 0.001) were all lower for LEP patients than for non-LEP patients, regardless of interpreter use (Fig. 1). In multivariate analysis, LEP patients were significantly less likely to have pain assessed ≥ 2 times: OR 0.63 (95% CI, 0.44–0.91) and OR 0.68 (95% CI 0.60–0.77) for LEP with and without language line interpreter use, respectively, compared to non-LEP.
intervention: "In this first multi-state evaluation of pain management by EMS among patients with and without LEP, we found pain to be assessed and treated significantly less often among LEP patients. Use of interpreter services did not ameliorate this disparity.

There are several potential explanations for our findings. LEP patients may feel less empowered and equipped to advocate for pain management. EMS providers may feel uncomfortable assessing and treating pain in patients with whom they have difficulty communicating. Moreover, while prior studies have not examined pain management by EMS among LEP patients specifically, there is robust evidence that individuals from racial/ethnic minoritized communities (many of whom may have LEP) are less likely to receive pain medications in the prehospital setting than white patients, likely due to bias and structural racism.5,6 While our results differ from the only prior study assessing EMS care in LEP patients4 — which found no difference in pain assessments or management — that study focused on a single center in a diverse setting where care pathways for LEP patients may be more established. Thus, further studies are needed to investigate treatment gaps in other settings and how to mitigate them."
